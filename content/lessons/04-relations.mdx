# Relations

A relation is a way to record **which ordered pairs satisfy some connection**. That is the central idea of this chapter.

Everything else here—reflexivity, symmetry, equivalence classes, partial orders, Hasse diagrams, inverse relations, composition, and closures—is a question about those ordered pairs.

This lesson builds on [Sets and Set Operations](../lessons/03-sets-and-set-operations.mdx), especially Cartesian products and partitions, and on [Predicates and Quantifiers](../lessons/02-predicates-and-quantifiers.mdx).

When a relation exercise feels abstract, return to one question:

> **Which ordered pairs are in the relation?**

That translation usually turns the notation back into something concrete.

## Relations as sets of ordered pairs

Suppose

$$
A=\{1,2\},\qquad B=\{u,v,w\}.
$$

Their Cartesian product is

$$
A\times B=\{(1,u),(1,v),(1,w),(2,u),(2,v),(2,w)\}.
$$

A **binary relation from $A$ to $B$** is any subset of $A\times B$.

For example,

$$
R=\{(1,u),(2,v),(2,w)\}
$$

is a relation from $A$ to $B$. It simply selects three of the six available ordered pairs.

The notation

$$
1Ru
$$

means exactly

$$
(1,u)\in R.
$$

Likewise, $1Rv$ is false because $(1,v)\notin R$.

So whenever $aRb$ feels unfamiliar, read it as:

> “The ordered pair $(a,b)$ belongs to $R$.”

### Relations are also two-input predicates

A relation does not have to be given by explicitly listing pairs. It may be defined by a rule.

For example, on the integers,

$$
aRb\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a<b.
$$

This says that $(a,b)$ belongs to the relation exactly when the statement $a<b$ is true.

So there are two equivalent viewpoints:

- a relation is a set of ordered pairs;
- a relation is a two-input predicate that is true for exactly those pairs.

In programming terms, you can think of these as roughly analogous to

```text
R(a, b) -> bool
```

and

```text
{ (a,b) : R(a,b) is true }
```

The notation $aRb$ is just infix notation, much like $a<b$ or $a\mid b$.

### Relations from $A$ to $B$ versus relations on $A$

A relation **from $A$ to $B$** is a subset

$$
R\subseteq A\times B.
$$

The first coordinate comes from $A$ and the second from $B$.

A relation **on $A$** is a subset

$$
R\subseteq A\times A.
$$

Both coordinates come from the same set.

Most of the properties in this chapter—reflexive, symmetric, antisymmetric, and transitive—are properties of relations **on one set**.

The underlying set matters. For example,

$$
R=\{(1,1)\}
$$

is reflexive on $\{1\}$, but not on $\{1,2\}$ because $(2,2)$ is missing.

That means a pair list by itself does not always tell you everything. You also need to know the set on which the relation is defined.

### Three ways to display the same relation

Suppose

$$
A=\{a,b,c\}
$$

and

$$
R=\{(a,b),(b,c),(c,c)\}.
$$

You can represent this same relation in three common ways.

**Ordered pairs:** simply list the members of $R$.

**Directed graph:** draw one vertex for each element. Draw an arrow $x\to y$ exactly when $(x,y)\in R$. A pair such as $(c,c)$ becomes a loop at $c$.

**Boolean matrix:** choose an order for the elements. Rows represent first coordinates and columns represent second coordinates. Put 1 when the pair belongs to the relation and 0 otherwise.

These are not three different mathematical objects. They are three representations of the same set of pairs.

A reliable translation procedure is:

1. Identify the underlying set or sets.
2. Identify the possible pairs in the relevant Cartesian product.
3. Determine which pairs are actually in $R$.
4. Translate those pairs into arrows or matrix entries if needed.

## Four fundamental properties

Let $R$ be a relation on a set $A$.

The four main properties ask four different questions:

- **Reflexive:** does every object relate to itself?
- **Symmetric:** can every existing pair be reversed?
- **Antisymmetric:** can two distinct objects relate in both directions?
- **Transitive:** does every two-step chain have its shortcut?

It is much easier to test them if you look for these pair patterns directly.

### Reflexive

$R$ is **reflexive** when

$$
\forall a\in A,\ aRa.
$$

In pair language:

$$
(a,a)\in R\quad\text{for every }a\in A.
$$

So if

$$
A=\{1,2,3\},
$$

reflexivity requires all three loops:

$$
(1,1),(2,2),(3,3).
$$

A single missing loop proves that the relation is not reflexive.

### Symmetric

$R$ is **symmetric** when

$$
\forall a,b\in A,\ aRb\to bRa.
$$

In pair language:

> If $(a,b)$ is present, then $(b,a)$ must also be present.

If $(1,2)$ occurs but $(2,1)$ does not, symmetry fails immediately.

Loops cause no problem: reversing $(a,a)$ gives the same pair.

### Antisymmetric

$R$ is **antisymmetric** when

$$
\forall a,b\in A,\ (aRb\land bRa)\to a=b.
$$

A practical reading is:

> If $a\ne b$, you may not have both $(a,b)$ and $(b,a)$.

Loops are allowed.

This is why **antisymmetric does not mean “not symmetric.”** Equality, for example, is both symmetric and antisymmetric: only loops occur.

A relation can also be neither symmetric nor antisymmetric.

### Transitive

$R$ is **transitive** when

$$
\forall a,b,c\in A,\ (aRb\land bRc)\to aRc.
$$

In pair language:

> If $(a,b)$ and $(b,c)$ are present, then $(a,c)$ must also be present.

The middle entries must match. That is what makes the two pairs a **composable two-step chain**.

For example,

$$
(1,2),(2,3)
$$

require

$$
(1,3).
$$

You do **not** test every permutation of the elements. You test the two-step chains that actually exist in the relation.

A systematic finite test is:

1. Pick a pair $(a,b)$ in $R$.
2. Find every pair in $R$ whose first coordinate is $b$.
3. For each $(b,c)$, check whether $(a,c)$ is present.
4. Repeat for every starting pair.

The variables may repeat. If $(a,b)$ and $(b,a)$ are both present, transitivity also requires $(a,a)$ and $(b,b)$.

### Do not confuse the names

The properties are independent conditions, not opposite labels.

“Not symmetric” does not imply antisymmetric. For example,

$$
R=\{(1,2),(2,1),(2,3)\}
$$

is not symmetric because $(3,2)$ is missing, and it is not antisymmetric because distinct elements 1 and 2 relate in both directions.

## A complete finite check

Let

$$
A=\{1,2,3\}
$$

and

$$
R=\{(1,1),(2,2),(3,3),(1,2),(2,3)\}.
$$

Test each property separately.

**Reflexive:** yes. Every required loop is present.

**Symmetric:** no. $(1,2)$ is present but $(2,1)$ is absent.

**Antisymmetric:** yes. No two distinct elements occur in both directions.

**Transitive:** no. We have $(1,2)$ and $(2,3)$, so transitivity requires $(1,3)$, which is missing.

<Figure id="relation-finite-check" alt="Inspecting the finite relation" />

Adding $(1,3)$ repairs that transitivity failure.

For a finite relation, this pair-pattern approach is safer than randomly trying triples. For an infinite relation, enumeration is impossible, so use the defining condition algebraically.

## Vacuous cases and related properties

Some relation properties are implications. If their antecedent never happens, there is nothing that can violate them.

For example, the empty relation on a nonempty set is symmetric, antisymmetric, and transitive. There are no pairs that could violate any of those implication-based conditions.

It is not reflexive on a nonempty set because reflexivity explicitly requires loops.

On the empty set, the empty relation is reflexive too: there is no element missing its loop.

### Irreflexive

$R$ is **irreflexive** when

$$
\forall a\in A,\ \neg aRa.
$$

Every loop must be absent.

Be careful:

- **not reflexive** means at least one required loop is missing;
- **irreflexive** means every loop is missing.

A relation with some loops but not all of them is neither reflexive nor irreflexive.

### Asymmetric

$R$ is **asymmetric** when

$$
aRb\to\neg bRa.
$$

If one direction exists, the reverse direction is forbidden.

Asymmetry also forbids loops. If $aRa$ were true, asymmetry would require $\neg aRa$. Therefore every asymmetric relation is automatically irreflexive.

The relation $<$ is asymmetric. The relation $\leq$ is antisymmetric but not asymmetric because $a\leq a$.

## Equivalence relations

An **equivalence relation** is a relation that is

- reflexive,
- symmetric,
- transitive.

It formalizes the idea that two objects are being treated as “the same” according to some chosen criterion.

Examples include:

- strings having the same length;
- people having the same birthday month;
- integers having the same remainder modulo $m$.

### Congruence modulo $m$

Fix a positive integer $m$. Define

$$
a\equiv b\pmod m
$$

when

$$
m\mid(a-b).
$$

Equivalently,

$$
aRb\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad m\mid(a-b).
$$

This means $a$ and $b$ differ by an integer multiple of $m$.

To prove that congruence modulo $m$ is an equivalence relation, check the three required properties.

**Reflexive:**

$$
a-a=0=m\cdot0.
$$

So $m\mid(a-a)$.

**Symmetric:** if

$$
a-b=mk,
$$

then

$$
b-a=m(-k).
$$

So divisibility still holds after reversing the difference.

**Transitive:** if

$$
a-b=mk
$$

and

$$
b-c=m\ell,
$$

then add the equations:

$$
a-c=m(k+\ell).
$$

Therefore $m\mid(a-c)$.

The important proof habit is to translate divisibility into an equation with an integer multiplier. That gives you algebra you can manipulate.

## Equivalence classes and partitions

Suppose $R$ is an equivalence relation on $A$.

The **equivalence class of $a$** is

$$
[a]=\{x\in A:xRa\}.
$$

In plain language:

> $[a]$ is the set of every element related to $a$.

The brackets do not mean “the element $a$.” They mean the whole group containing $a$.

For congruence modulo 3,

$$
[0]=\{3k:k\in\mathbb Z\},\qquad
[1]=\{3k+1:k\in\mathbb Z\},\qquad
[2]=\{3k+2:k\in\mathbb Z\}.
$$

A **representative** is simply one member used to name the class. Thus

$$
[1]=[4]=[7].
$$

Those are not three different classes. They are three names for the same class.

### Why $aRb$ implies $[a]=[b]$

Suppose

$$
aRb.
$$

To prove the sets $[a]$ and $[b]$ are equal, use the usual set-equality strategy: prove both inclusions.

For $[a]\subseteq[b]$, take an arbitrary $x\in[a]$. By definition,

$$
xRa.
$$

We are given $aRb$, so transitivity gives

$$
xRb.
$$

Therefore $x\in[b]$.

For the reverse direction, symmetry turns $aRb$ into $bRa$, and the same argument gives $[b]\subseteq[a]$.

Therefore

$$
[a]=[b].
$$

This explains a fundamental fact: equivalence classes can never partly overlap. If two classes share an element, they are actually the same class.

### From an equivalence relation to a partition

The distinct equivalence classes form a partition of $A$.

- They are nonempty because reflexivity gives $aRa$, so $a\in[a]$.
- They cover $A$ because every element belongs to its own class.
- They are pairwise disjoint unless they are the same class.

So an equivalence relation groups the set into nonoverlapping blocks.

### From a partition to an equivalence relation

This direction is easy to state but needs an explicit construction.

Suppose a partition of $A$ has blocks

$$
B_1,B_2,\ldots,B_n.
$$

Define

$$
aRb
$$

exactly when $a$ and $b$ belong to the same block.

If an exercise asks you to **list the pairs in the induced equivalence relation**, do this:

1. Take one block $B_i$.
2. Form $B_i\times B_i$.
3. Every pair in that Cartesian product belongs to the relation.
4. Repeat for each block.
5. Take the union of all those pair sets.

Symbolically,

$$
R=(B_1\times B_1)\cup(B_2\times B_2)\cup\cdots\cup(B_n\times B_n).
$$

#### Worked example

Suppose the partition is

$$
\big\{\{a,c\},\{b\}\big\}.
$$

The block $\{a,c\}$ contributes

$$
\{a,c\}\times\{a,c\}
=
\{(a,a),(a,c),(c,a),(c,c)\}.
$$

The block $\{b\}$ contributes

$$
\{b\}\times\{b\}=\{(b,b)\}.
$$

So the induced relation is the union of those pair sets.

The rule “same block” is automatically reflexive, symmetric, and transitive:

- every element is in the same block as itself;
- if $a$ shares a block with $b$, then $b$ shares that block with $a$;
- if $a$ shares a block with $b$ and $b$ shares a block with $c$, then all three are in the same block.

This gives the two-way correspondence:

> equivalence relation $\longleftrightarrow$ partition into equivalence classes.

## Partial and total orders

An equivalence relation groups objects that count as the same. A **partial order** does something different: it organizes objects by a consistent notion of “below,” “before,” “contained in,” or “divides.”

A **partial order** is a relation that is

- reflexive,
- antisymmetric,
- transitive.

A set together with a partial order is a **partially ordered set**, or **poset**.

Notice the key difference from an equivalence relation:

- equivalence relation: reflexive + **symmetric** + transitive;
- partial order: reflexive + **antisymmetric** + transitive.

### Example: subset inclusion

On $\mathcal P(S)$, the relation $\subseteq$ is a partial order.

- Reflexive: $A\subseteq A$.
- Antisymmetric: if $A\subseteq B$ and $B\subseteq A$, then $A=B$.
- Transitive: if $A\subseteq B$ and $B\subseteq C$, then $A\subseteq C$.

### Example: divisibility

On the positive integers, divisibility is a partial order.

- Reflexive: $a\mid a$ because $a=a\cdot1$.
- Antisymmetric: if $a\mid b$ and $b\mid a$ with positive integers, then $a=b$.
- Transitive: if $a\mid b$ and $b\mid c$, then $a\mid c$.

The positive-domain restriction matters. On all integers, $2\mid-2$ and $-2\mid2$, but $2\ne-2$, so antisymmetry fails.

### Comparable and incomparable elements

Two elements $a,b$ are **comparable** when

$$
a\preceq b\quad\text{or}\quad b\preceq a.
$$

A partial order does **not** require every pair to be comparable.

For example, under subset inclusion,

$$
\{1\}\nsubseteq\{2\}
$$

and

$$
\{2\}\nsubseteq\{1\}.
$$

So $\{1\}$ and $\{2\}$ are incomparable.

A partial order becomes a **total order** when every pair is comparable.

Numerical $\leq$ on $\mathbb Z$ is a total order. Subset inclusion on a nontrivial power set is usually only partial.

## Hasse diagrams and extreme elements

A finite partial order can contain many pairs because reflexivity contributes every loop and transitivity contributes many implied comparisons.

A **Hasse diagram** is a compressed picture of the order. It deliberately leaves out information that can be recovered automatically.

To build one, start from the partial order and remove two kinds of edges:

1. **Remove loops.** Reflexivity is understood, so $(a,a)$ is not drawn.
2. **Remove transitive shortcuts.** If $a\prec b\prec c$, the direct comparison $a\prec c$ is already implied by the upward path through $b$, so do not draw a direct edge from $a$ to $c$.

What remains are the **cover relations**.

Write $a\prec b$ when $a\preceq b$ and $a\ne b$. An element $b$ **[covers](ref:cover)** $a$ when

$$
a\prec b
$$

and there is no $c$ with

$$
a\prec c\prec b.
$$

Draw $b$ above $a$ when $b$ [covers](ref:cover?repeat) $a$.

### Why a chain can represent more comparisons than it draws

Suppose the order contains

$$
1\preceq2,\qquad2\preceq3,\qquad1\preceq3.
$$

A Hasse diagram draws only

$$
1-2-3
$$

vertically, with 1 at the bottom and 3 at the top.

It does **not** draw a direct 1-to-3 edge because transitivity already lets you recover $1\preceq3$ from the path $1\to2\to3$.

This is why you should not think of a Hasse diagram as “the list of relation pairs.” It is a compressed representation from which the full partial order can be reconstructed.

To decide whether $a\preceq b$ from a Hasse diagram:

- if $a=b$, yes by reflexivity;
- otherwise, see whether there is an upward path from $a$ to $b$.

You do not test arbitrary permutations of the vertices. Direction matters: upward paths encode the order.

Under inclusion, $\mathcal P(\{1,2\})$ has $\varnothing$ at the bottom, $\{1\}$ and $\{2\}$ in the middle, and $\{1,2\}$ at the top. The diagram is a diamond.

### Least versus minimal

A **least element** $\ell$ satisfies

$$
\ell\preceq x
$$

for **every** $x$ in the poset.

A **minimal element** merely has nothing strictly below it.

So:

- least means “below everybody”;
- minimal means “nothing is below me.”

A least element, if it exists, is unique. But there may be several minimal elements if they are incomparable.

Under divisibility on $\{2,3,6\}$, both 2 and 3 are minimal. Neither is least because neither divides the other. The element 6 is greatest.

The dual distinction is:

- **greatest:** above every element;
- **maximal:** nothing is strictly above it.

## Inverses and composition of relations

### Inverse relation

If

$$
R\subseteq A\times B,
$$

then the **inverse relation** is

$$
R^{-1}=\{(b,a):(a,b)\in R\}.
$$

You obtain it by reversing every ordered pair.

For example, if

$$
R=\{(a,1),(a,2),(b,2)\},
$$

then

$$
R^{-1}=\{(1,a),(2,a),(2,b)\}.
$$

For a relation on one set, symmetry can be written succinctly as

$$
R=R^{-1}.
$$

### Composition

Suppose

$$
R\subseteq A\times B
$$

and

$$
S\subseteq B\times C.
$$

The composition $S\circ R$ means **first follow $R$, then follow $S$**.

Formally,

$$
(a,c)\in S\circ R
$$

exactly when there exists some $b\in B$ such that

$$
(a,b)\in R
$$

and

$$
(b,c)\in S.
$$

The middle value $b$ is the connection point.

A practical procedure is:

1. Pick a pair $(a,b)$ from $R$.
2. Find every pair $(b,c)$ in $S$ whose first coordinate matches that $b$.
3. Add $(a,c)$ to $S\circ R$.
4. Repeat for every pair in $R$.
5. Remember that a relation is a set, so duplicate resulting pairs are listed only once.

Example:

$$
R=\{(1,u),(2,u),(2,v)\}
$$

and

$$
S=\{(u,p),(v,q)\}.
$$

The composable chains are

$$
1\to u\to p,
$$

$$
2\to u\to p,
$$

and

$$
2\to v\to q.
$$

Therefore

$$
S\circ R=\{(1,p),(2,p),(2,q)\}.
$$

### Composition explains transitivity

For a relation $R$ on $A$,

$$
R\circ R
$$

contains exactly the pairs reachable by a two-step $R$-chain.

Therefore transitivity is equivalent to

$$
R\circ R\subseteq R.
$$

Every two-step connection must already have its direct shortcut in $R$.

## Building closures without losing the meaning

A **closure** starts with a relation and adds the minimum pairs needed to force a chosen property while keeping every original pair.

Suppose

$$
A=\{a,b,c\}
$$

and

$$
R=\{(a,b),(b,c)\}.
$$

### Reflexive closure

Add every missing loop:

$$
(a,a),(b,b),(c,c).
$$

### Symmetric closure

Add the reverse of every existing pair:

$$
(b,a),(c,b).
$$

### Transitive closure

Add every shortcut required by existing chains. Here

$$
(a,b),(b,c)
$$

require

$$
(a,c).
$$

For larger finite relations, adding one shortcut may create a new chain that requires another shortcut. Continue until no new pairs are required.

The **transitive closure** can also be understood as adding $(a,b)$ whenever there is a path of one or more $R$-steps from $a$ to $b$.

The **reflexive-transitive closure** also includes every loop, corresponding to allowing a path of length zero.

### Closures can interact

If you want the smallest **equivalence relation** containing a relation, you need reflexivity, symmetry, and transitivity all at once.

Starting with

$$
\{(a,b),(b,c)\},
$$

adding reverses creates more chains, and those chains force still more pairs. Eventually all three elements become one equivalence class, so every pair in $A\times A$ is present.

This is why “take the closure” is not always a one-pass operation.

### Some properties cannot be repaired by only adding pairs

Suppose a relation already contains both

$$
(a,b)
$$

and

$$
(b,a)
$$

for distinct $a,b$.

Antisymmetry is already violated. Adding more pairs cannot undo that violation. You would have to remove a pair or change the objects or relation being modeled.

## Readiness check

Before moving on, you should be able to answer not only definitions but also the operational questions the exercises ask.

You should be able to:

- Translate $aRb$ into $(a,b)\in R$ and back.
- Distinguish a relation from $A$ to $B$ from a relation on $A$.
- Move between pair lists, directed graphs, and Boolean matrices.
- Test reflexive, symmetric, antisymmetric, and transitive by looking for the required pair patterns.
- Explain why transitivity checks composable chains rather than arbitrary permutations.
- Prove simple relation properties from algebraic definitions such as divisibility.
- Construct an equivalence class from its definition.
- Prove equality of equivalence classes by proving both set inclusions.
- Convert an equivalence relation into its partition of classes.
- Convert a partition into its induced relation by taking $B\times B$ for each block.
- Distinguish partial orders from equivalence relations.
- Distinguish comparable from incomparable elements.
- Read a Hasse diagram as a compressed partial order and recover implied comparisons from upward paths.
- Distinguish minimal from least and maximal from greatest.
- Reverse a relation to form $R^{-1}$.
- Compute $S\circ R$ by matching the middle coordinates.
- Build reflexive, symmetric, and transitive closures.

### A general strategy for relation exercises

When an exercise feels unclear, identify **what representation you are given** and **what representation you are supposed to produce**.

Examples:

- Given a pair set and asked about a property: inspect pair patterns.
- Given a graph and asked for the relation: turn each arrow into an ordered pair.
- Given a matrix and asked for pairs: every 1 becomes one ordered pair.
- Given a partition and asked for the induced equivalence relation: form $B\times B$ inside each block.
- Given an equivalence relation and asked for classes: collect all elements related to each representative.
- Given a partial order and asked for a Hasse diagram: remove loops and transitive shortcuts.
- Given a Hasse diagram and asked whether $a\preceq b$: look for an upward path from $a$ to $b$.
- Given two relations and asked for composition: match the second coordinate of the first relation to the first coordinate of the second.

If you can translate the problem back into ordered pairs, the notation usually stops being mysterious.
